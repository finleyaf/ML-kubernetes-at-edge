\chapter{Results, Evaluation and Discussion}
\label{chapter4}

This chapter interprets the outcome of the validated online pipeline described in Chapter~\ref{chapter3}. It reports the final authoritative online comparison from the Stage B experiment, in which the baseline arm was scheduled by the unmodified \texttt{default-scheduler}, while the custom arm used the locked safety-first ranker defined in Chapter~\ref{chapter2} and implemented in Chapter~\ref{chapter3}. The results are taken from the final \texttt{stage\_b\_summary.json} and interpreted against the hypotheses from Chapter~\ref{chapter2}. Where relevant, the discussion refers to the completed Pi-inclusive extension only as supplementary limitation evidence rather than as part of the claim-bearing hypothesis test.


\section{Online Experiment Results}
\label{sec:results}

The authoritative run comprised 10 trials, 12 pods per arm per trial, and 120 pods per arm overall. The aggregate claims below therefore rest on the same feasible worker set, the same workload mix, and the same counterbalanced trial order whose readiness and integrity were established in Chapter~\ref{chapter3}.

All reported means and rates are descriptive aggregates over these 10 matched trials. Given that scale, they are interpreted here as controlled comparative evidence rather than as formal inferential estimates.

Placement fairness and capacity alignment both declined slightly, but both arms remained highly balanced overall and the utility score increased substantially from 16.23 to 25.77, indicating that the safety and timing gains outweighed the small loss in evenness.

\subsection{Per-Decision Diagnostics}

The decision-level artifacts connect the aggregate gains back to actual ranker behaviour:

\begin{itemize}
\item The custom arm achieved a 100\% expected-node match rate, so every pod was bound to the node chosen by the ranker.
\item Prediction provenance was \texttt{model: 120}, showing that no custom-arm decision depended solely on fallback estimation in the final run.
\item Mean decision score was 0.3627, with mean predicted load 0.3818, mean base predicted load 0.2633, mean anomaly risk 0.0008, and mean capacity penalty 0.1184.
\item Stress-target avoidance improved from 84.44\% in the baseline arm to 87.78\% in the custom arm.
\end{itemize}

The low mean anomaly risk indicates that the deployed policy behaved primarily as a prediction-led safety-first ranker, with anomaly monitoring acting as a corrective term rather than dominating every decision. The non-zero capacity penalty and the lower chosen relative CPU show that workload request context and observed load guardrails were both materially involved in the placements.


\section{Hypothesis Evaluation}
\label{sec:hypothesis-eval}

This section evaluates each hypothesis from Chapter~\ref{chapter2} against the final online evidence.

\subsection{H1: Placement Quality}

\textbf{Accepted.} The custom scheduler improved the primary placement-quality metrics. Safe placement rate increased from 64.17\% to 75.83\%, anomalous placement rate decreased from 35.83\% to 24.17\%, and high-contention rate decreased from 56.67\% to 46.67\%. The gains were strongest under mixed and memory stress, with only one local reversal under \texttt{stress\_cpu} contention.


\subsection{H2: Scheduling Overhead}

\textbf{Accepted.} The custom scheduler did not introduce practically significant overhead. Mean scheduling latency was identical in both arms at 0.0667 seconds, while startup and total times improved by about 0.49 seconds each. The custom arm therefore achieved better placements without paying a measurable latency penalty at scheduling time.


\subsection{H3: Offline-to-Online Transfer}

\textbf{Accepted with qualification.} The locked hybrid family transferred successfully from offline audit to online deployment: utility improved from 16.23 to 25.77, safe placements increased by 14 decisions, and every custom-arm placement was generated by the intended model-backed ranking path. The qualification is that this transfer was demonstrated only on the claim-bearing worker set used in the final online comparison.


\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Effectiveness of the Hybrid Approach}

The final evidence suggests that the custom scheduler's benefit arises from a combination of prediction-led ranking, workload-aware capacity pressure, and explicit observed-load guardrails. Mean anomaly risk remained very low, so the anomaly term was not the dominant driver of most decisions. Instead, the decision logs show a policy that used prediction and request-aware capacity scaling as its primary ranking surface, while anomaly monitoring and unsafe-node penalties acted as protective corrections.

This interpretation is consistent with the aggregate outcome metrics. The custom arm reduced mean chosen relative CPU, improved stress-target avoidance, and improved safe placement without increasing scheduler latency. In other words, the system helped not by making scheduling slower or more complex, but by making the pre-binding choice better informed.


\subsection{Pi-Inclusive Extension}

A supplementary Pi-inclusive Stage C extension was completed after the main Stage B protocol had been fixed. In that extension, standard workloads remained restricted to the three cloud workers, while only the lighter Pi-fit workloads were allowed to consider \texttt{raspberrypi}. The extension did not support a positive heterogeneous transfer claim. The baseline arm placed 40 of 240 pods on the Pi (16.67\%), whereas the custom arm placed only 2 of 240 (0.83\%). Relative to the baseline arm, the custom arm's utility fell by 13.10 and its high-contention rate increased by 22.92 percentage points.

This completed extension is therefore interpreted as limitation evidence rather than as contradictory evidence against the main claim. A plausible reason for the failure is that the retained policy was selected and locked on the homogeneous cloud worker setting, where predictor behaviour, anomaly history, and safety penalties were calibrated against a relatively consistent worker class. The Raspberry Pi introduced a qualitatively different capacity and performance profile, so even lite workloads did not necessarily appear attractive once the ranker combined predicted load, observed contention, and safety-first guardrails. In effect, the custom arm transferred conservatively: when faced with a worker whose behaviour differed from the policy selection surface, it tended to avoid that node rather than exploit it. This points to a constructive direction for future work. Positive heterogeneous transfer will likely require node-class-aware calibration, hardware normalisation, or predictors and penalty terms that are explicitly conditioned on worker type rather than inherited unchanged from the homogeneous setting.


\subsection{Comparison with Related Work}

The result is consistent with the broader literature on dynamic edge scheduling. Liu et al.~argue that Kubernetes scheduling in collaborative edge environments benefits from load-aware resource optimisation beyond static requests \cite{liu2024dynamic}. Rasouli et al.~show that failure- and latency-aware decision surfaces matter for edge reliability \cite{rasouli2025false}. Fritzsche et al.~demonstrate that lightweight anomaly detection is practical on edge Kubernetes clusters \cite{fritzsche2024lightweight}. The present work combines those ideas in a lightweight form: anomaly awareness is retained, but it is coupled to short-horizon prediction and workload-aware placement rather than used as a standalone warning signal.

Compared with heavier approaches such as reinforcement-learning schedulers or deep recurrent predictors, the implemented system remains lightweight enough to operate on one-second telemetry without a dedicated training service or large inference budget. That keeps it closer to the deployment constraints expected in edge settings, even if it may sacrifice some expressive power relative to larger models.


\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Several limitations remain.

\begin{itemize}
\item The cluster is still modest relative to production edge deployments, so the result should be interpreted as controlled evidence rather than as a universal scheduler verdict.
\item The live experiment relies on controlled stress injection rather than organic failure cascades, network disruption, or long-running multi-tenant interference.
\item The authoritative comparison is restricted to a homogeneous worker subset. This improves internal validity for the baseline comparison, and the completed Pi-inclusive extension shows that positive heterogeneous transfer is not yet demonstrated by the current ranker.
\item The predictor remains a lightweight linear family and may underfit more complex mixed-resource or long-horizon behaviour.
\item Placement fairness and capacity alignment declined slightly under the custom arm, showing that the safety-first policy accepts a small trade-off in evenness to avoid busier nodes.
\end{itemize}


\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

The online experiment answers the three research questions positively within the scope of the claim-bearing comparison. For \textbf{RQ1}, the custom anomaly-aware, prediction-informed scheduler improved placement quality by increasing safe placements and reducing anomalous and highly contended decisions. For \textbf{RQ2}, these gains were achieved without measurable scheduling latency overhead and with lower startup and total execution times. For \textbf{RQ3}, the locked hybrid policy family transferred successfully from offline audit to online deployment, as shown by the positive utility delta and the fully traceable model-backed decision path.

Taken together, these results support the central claim of the project: a lightweight scheduler that combines short-horizon prediction, anomaly awareness, and explicit live safety guardrails can outperform the default Kubernetes scheduler on relevant placement outcomes in a controlled edge-style setting. A completed supplementary Pi-inclusive extension exposed a remaining limitation, however: the current safety-first ranker does not yet translate that advantage into effective use of a mixed-capacity worker pool.


\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future-work}

The most important next steps are:

\begin{itemize}
\item redesign the heterogeneous ranking logic for mixed-capacity workers, then rerun the Pi-inclusive extension with workload-specific eligibility and a defensible common feasible set
\item extend the campaign to larger worker sets and longer runs for tighter uncertainty estimates
\item evaluate richer predictors for mixed-resource regimes while preserving lightweight deployment constraints
\item introduce more organic fault, anomaly, and network-disruption scenarios
\item integrate the ranking logic more deeply with native Kubernetes scheduling interfaces while retaining per-decision audit artifacts
\end{itemize}
